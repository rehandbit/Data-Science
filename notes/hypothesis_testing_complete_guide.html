What even is a hypothesis test?

▼

Imagine you work at a company and someone says: "Our new website design gets more clicks than the old one." That is a claim. But how do you know it's actually true and not just random chance?

A hypothesis test is a structured method to decide whether a claim about data is real, or just happened by luck. It uses maths to answer: "If nothing actually changed, how likely is it that I'd see results this extreme?"

Real-world analogy — court of law

In a court, a person is innocent until proven guilty. You start by assuming innocence (nothing changed), then look at evidence. Only if the evidence is overwhelmingly against innocence do you convict (reject the assumption). Hypothesis testing works exactly the same way.

Say this in an interview
"Hypothesis testing is a statistical framework to decide whether observed data provides enough evidence to reject a default assumption. We start by assuming nothing is different — the null hypothesis — and then calculate the probability of seeing our data if that assumption were true. If that probability is very low, we reject the null and accept there is a real effect."
Step 1

Null hypothesis (H₀) vs alternative hypothesis (H₁)

▼

Every hypothesis test starts with two competing statements about the world:

H₀ — null hypothesis
No effect. Nothing changed.
The default, boring, skeptical assumption. Like "innocent until proven guilty." You assume this is true until evidence says otherwise.
e.g. "The new button colour has no effect on click rate."
H₁ — alternative hypothesis
There IS an effect.
This is what you want to prove. You only accept it if the data provides strong enough evidence to reject H₀.
e.g. "The new button colour increases click rate."
Critical point to memorise
You never "prove" the null hypothesis. You either reject H₀ (evidence is strong enough) or fail to reject H₀ (evidence is not strong enough). You never say "H₀ is true" — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
Say this in an interview
"The null hypothesis is the default assumption of no effect — for example, 'this drug has no impact on blood pressure'. The alternative is what we're trying to demonstrate. We never directly test the alternative; instead, we try to disprove the null. If the evidence makes the null extremely unlikely, we reject it and accept the alternative. Importantly, failing to reject the null doesn't mean it's true — it just means our evidence wasn't strong enough."
Step 2

P-value — the most important number

▼

The p-value answers one very specific question:

"If the null hypothesis were true, what is the probability of getting results at least this extreme?"
Small p-value (e.g. 0.02)
Rare result under H₀
Only a 2% chance of seeing this if nothing changed. This is suspicious — maybe something did change. Reject H₀.
Large p-value (e.g. 0.45)
Common result under H₀
45% chance of seeing this even if nothing changed. This is totally normal. Don't reject H₀.
Simple analogy — coin flip

You flip a coin 10 times. H₀: "The coin is fair." You get 9 heads. The p-value asks: if the coin were fair, how likely is getting 9+ heads? The answer is about 1% — very unlikely. So you reject H₀ and conclude the coin is probably biased.

The biggest misconception — interviewers love testing this
A p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true. It is the probability of your data, assuming H₀ is true. This is a subtle but critical difference. p = 0.03 does not mean "there's a 3% chance nothing changed" — it means "if nothing changed, there's a 3% chance of seeing results this extreme."
Say this in an interview
"The p-value is the probability of observing results at least as extreme as ours, assuming the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value — typically below 0.05 — means our results would be very unlikely under the null, so we reject it. It's important to clarify that the p-value is not the probability that the null is true; it's a measure of how surprising our data is under the null assumption."
Step 3

Significance level (α) — where you draw the line

▼

Before running your test, you decide a threshold called the significance level (α). This is the p-value cutoff below which you'll reject H₀. It's your definition of "surprising enough."

α = 0.05
Most common
Used in most business & social science. 5% false alarm rate accepted.
α = 0.01
Stricter
Used in medicine & safety. Only 1% false alarm rate allowed.
α = 0.001
Very strict
Used in physics (e.g. CERN particle detection). Near-zero false alarms.
Decision rule: if p-value < α → reject H₀ (statistically significant)
if p-value ≥ α → fail to reject H₀ (not significant)
Why 0.05? It's a convention, not a law. Ronald Fisher suggested it in the 1920s as a rough guide. In high-stakes domains like clinical trials, much lower α values are required. In exploratory data analysis, 0.1 might be acceptable. Always set α before you see the data — changing it afterwards is p-hacking.
Say this in an interview
"The significance level α is the threshold we set before the test — typically 0.05. If the p-value falls below α, we call the result statistically significant and reject the null. It represents the maximum false-positive rate we're willing to tolerate. The choice of α depends on context — medical trials often use 0.01 or lower because the cost of a false positive is much higher."
Step 4

Type I and Type II errors — the two ways to be wrong

▼

No test is perfect. There are two ways a hypothesis test can make a mistake:

H₀ is actually TRUE
H₀ is actually FALSE
You reject H₀
Type I Error ✗
False positive. You said there's an effect — there isn't.
Correct ✓
True positive. You correctly detected a real effect.
You don't reject H₀
Correct ✓
True negative. Correctly found no effect.
Type II Error ✗
False negative. You missed a real effect.
Type I error rate = α
False positive
Rejecting H₀ when it's actually true. Probability = α. You control this by setting α.
e.g. Concluding a drug works when it doesn't.
Type II error rate = β
False negative
Failing to reject H₀ when it's actually false. Reduced by increasing sample size.
e.g. Concluding a drug doesn't work when it does.
Power = 1 − β. Statistical power is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect. High power is good. You increase power by collecting more data (larger sample size), which shrinks β without changing α.
Say this in an interview
"A Type I error is a false positive — rejecting the null when it's actually true. Its probability equals α, so setting α = 0.05 means we accept a 5% chance of a Type I error. A Type II error is a false negative — missing a real effect. Its probability is β, and 1 minus β is called statistical power. There's a trade-off: making α smaller reduces Type I errors but increases Type II errors unless you compensate with a larger sample size."
Worked example

Full A/B test — step by step

▼

Scenario: You changed the colour of a "Buy Now" button from grey to orange. You want to know if this increased the click-through rate.

1
State the hypotheses
H₀: The orange button has no effect on click rate (p_orange = p_grey)
H₁: The orange button has a higher click rate (p_orange > p_grey)
2
Choose significance level
α = 0.05. Standard choice for a business experiment.
3
Collect data
Grey button: 1000 visitors, 50 clicks (5% CTR)
Orange button: 1000 visitors, 70 clicks (7% CTR)
4
Run the statistical test
Use a two-proportion z-test. The test calculates how many standard deviations the observed difference is from zero (the null). Result: p-value = 0.018
5
Make the decision
p = 0.018 < α = 0.05 → Reject H₀
The orange button's higher CTR is statistically significant. Ship it.
Say this as the conclusion
"With a p-value of 0.018, which is below our significance threshold of 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis. The evidence suggests the orange button genuinely increases click-through rate and the result is unlikely to be due to random chance alone. However, we should also check the practical significance — a 2% absolute lift — and whether that is worth deploying."
Don't forget — statistical significance ≠ practical significance
With millions of users, even a 0.001% improvement can be statistically significant (tiny p-value), but it might not be worth engineering effort. Always report effect size alongside p-value.
